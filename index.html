Part 1

What is a dystopia?

the genre you are studying

Dystopia

  • A dystopia is a fictional, futuristic society with great suffering and injustice.
  • The word means “bad place”.
  • Its opposite is a utopia: an imagined place where everything is fair and perfect.

Why do writers create dystopias?

  • To illuminate (shine a light on) suffering and injustice in our own society.
  • They exaggerate real problems, such as inequality, racism, war, climate destruction or the treatment of workers.
  • They want readers to reflect, and to change things for the better.

The six conventions of a dystopia

ConventionWhat it looks like
SufferingPeople are hungry, ill, exhausted or in pain.
InjusticeLife is unfair: a powerful few live well while most people struggle.
Fearful citizensPeople are scared, watched and afraid to speak.
Lack of freedomRules, cameras, guards and orders control every move.
Destruction of naturePollution, smog, dead crops and ruined land.
RebellionSomeone questions the system, or fights back.

Example: The Hunger Games

  • By Suzanne Collins.
  • Children from poor districts are forced to fight in brutal games, watched for entertainment by the rich in the Capitol.
  • It highlights inequality between rich and poor.

Example: Noughts and Crosses

  • By Malorie Blackman.
  • Two racial groups: the Crosses have power and wealth, while the Noughts face poverty and discrimination.
  • It exposes the damage caused by racism.
Remember: a dystopian writer is always saying something about our world. That is the “why” you can write about in your answer.

Part 2

Setting and atmosphere

how dystopian writers make the world feel

In a dystopia, the setting often reflects how characters feel. If you can explain the atmosphere, you can explain how it affects the character.

BleakThere is little or no hope.
OppressiveHarsh and controlling: it makes you feel worried and trapped.
UnsettlingIt leaves you feeling anxious.
HostileUnfriendly and threatening.

Methods writers use

“The clouds scowled down, unleashing rain that fell like the tears of a world gone awry.”

Personification and simile

Personification: the clouds scowl like an angry person, so the setting feels hostile, as if it is attacking the people below.

“The windows, like watchful eyes, seemed to follow my every move”

Simile

The simile makes the buildings seem to spy on the speaker. This creates an oppressive, watched atmosphere.

“the buildings loomed above like the skeletal remnants of a nightmare”

Simile

Skeletal links the city to death. Loomed makes the buildings feel threatening.

“The taunting clouds began to weep a brutal, unforgiving rain which stung the bare arms of the citizens below.”

Semantic field of cruelty

A semantic field is a group of linked words. Taunting, brutal, unforgiving, stung all suggest cruelty, creating an oppressive tone.

Big idea: a hostile setting often shows how powerless and trapped the characters are.

Part 3

Characters in dystopian texts

the types of character you might meet

Your question will be about a character. Most dystopian characters fit one of these types.

1

The questioner or rebel

  • Starts to see that something is wrong, and asks questions.
  • May hide their feelings, or fight back.
  • Usually the protagonist.
2

The suffering citizen

  • Hungry, exhausted, frightened or hopeless.
  • Shows the reader how cruel the society is.
3

The obedient citizen

  • Follows the rules without question.
  • Shows how the society controls people’s minds.
4

The oppressor

  • Holds power: a leader, guard or system.
  • Callous, controlling and often watching.

How writers present a character

What they look like

  • Adjectives and noun phrases: an emaciated, hunched woman.
  • Their body often shows their suffering.

What they do

  • Verbs: trudged, peered, concealed.
  • Actions show feelings the character may not say out loud.

What they think and feel

  • Questions (interrogative sentences) show doubt: Had we never been free?
  • Exclamatory sentences show strong feeling: It was impossible!

The world around them

  • The setting reflects their mood.
  • Cameras and orders show they are watched and controlled.
Remember: always ask why the writer presents the character this way. What does the character show us about the dystopian world, and about our own?

Part 4

Practise on an extract

three short passages to try

The real extract will be added soon. Until then, practise the skill on these passages from your lessons. For each one, ask: how is the character presented?

Passage A: Donna

The once vibrant city now lay in ruins: the air was thick with toxic fumes, the houses were reduced to rubble and the citizens were ghosts of their former selves. Donna, an emaciated, hunched woman, peered out of the window of the skyscraper in which she worked. She watched as people wandered aimlessly through the dingy, desolate streets. What had happened to the city she knew and loved? Whilst trapped in her reverie, she caught sight of a battered billboard and it pictured a red, penetrating eye. Graffiti sprawled over it now which was bold, unruly, defiant. With this realisation, Donna smiled to herself. Perhaps there was a way out of this nightmare. Quickly, she concealed her smile and resumed her work.

Example passage from Oak National Academy

Things to notice

  • emaciated, hunched: she is starving and worn down.
  • What had happened to the city she knew and loved? A question shows she is starting to doubt the regime.
  • bold, unruly, defiant: the graffiti gives her hope of rebellion.
  • concealed her smile: she must hide her feelings, so she is watched and not free.

How is Donna presented?

  • Suffering, but secretly hopeful.
  • A questioner who may become a rebel.
  • Careful and afraid: she hides her hope.

Passage B: the man

His city was in ruin. The air was thick with acrid haze and the buildings loomed above like the skeletal remnants of a nightmare. Abandoned cars lined the streets, their windows shattered and their tyres deflated. The defiant graffiti, sprawled boldly on the walls, was the only thing of colour. Inconspicuous cameras hovered everywhere, sharp like hawks. Had breathing always been so difficult? Had this place always been so bleak? Had we never been free? he questioned. A shrill alarm sounded which instantaneously erased his mind of these questions and speculations, though small remnants of suspicion remained. This ‘mind-wiping’ happened daily. He was beginning to despair. It was impossible! He would never find out the truth!

Example passage from Oak National Academy

Things to notice

  • Three questions in a row show his growing suspicion.
  • mind-wiping: the regime controls even his thoughts.
  • Exclamations at the end show his despair.

How is he presented?

  • Confused and controlled.
  • Hopeless, but a little suspicious: small remnants of suspicion remained.

Passage C: the sunken woman

A sunken woman trudged home along the dusty streets. She wore frayed clothing, coated with grease and sweat that gaped around her emaciated body. Most people thought about what they might have for dinner on the way home from work but her only thought was if there would be any. The forests were scant. Crops had dried up, wildlife had fled and she could not risk asking a neighbour for food. It was barren land full of barren hearts.

Example passage from Oak National Academy

Things to notice

  • sunken, emaciated, frayed: her body and clothes show her poverty.
  • She cannot even risk asking for food: people are fearful and isolated.
  • barren land full of barren hearts: the land and the people are both empty.

How is she presented?

  • Suffering and starving.
  • Isolated and afraid.
  • A victim of an unjust society.

Part 5

Planning lesson: annotating the extract

what to do in your first lesson

Your assessment has a planning lesson first. Use it well: a good plan makes the writing lesson much easier.

  1. Read the question and underline the character you are writing about.
  2. Read the bullet-point prompts. They tell you what to cover.
  3. Read the extract once, all the way through.
  4. Highlight 4 or 5 short quotations about the character: how they look, what they do, what they think and feel.
  5. Annotate each quotation: name the method (simile, verb, adjective, question...) and write what it suggests about the character.
  6. Zoom in: circle one powerful word in each quotation.
  7. Write the big idea at the top: what does this character show about the dystopian world? Why might the writer have created them?
  8. Number your quotations in the order you will write about them.
Open book: you do not need to learn quotations. Use the extract. Your job is to explain them.

Part 6

Writing lesson: your answer with PEEL

how is the character presented?

Write in paragraphs. Use PEEL, as you do in class.

PPointOne idea about the character. Donna is presented as secretly hopeful.
EEvidenceA short quotation, embedded in your sentence. Donna “smiled to herself” when she saw the graffiti.
EExplainName the method, zoom in on one word, and use because. The adjective “defiant” suggests resistance, because...
LLinkLink to the big idea and the writer’s purpose. Use may, could, perhaps. Perhaps the writer suggests that hope survives even under oppression.

Model paragraph 1: How is Donna presented?

The writer presents Donna as worn down by the dystopian society. She is described as “an emaciated, hunched woman”. The adjective “emaciated” suggests that she is starving, because it means extremely thin, while “hunched” makes her seem exhausted and defeated. Perhaps the writer does this to show how the regime causes suffering, so that the reader feels sympathy for ordinary citizens.

Model paragraph 2: However...

However, Donna is also presented as secretly hopeful. When she sees graffiti that is “bold, unruly, defiant”, she “smiled to herself”. The list of three adjectives suggests that someone is resisting the regime, and her smile shows this gives her hope. In particular, the verb “concealed” shows that she must hide her feelings, because she is being watched. The writer may be suggesting that even in an oppressive society, people cannot be completely controlled.

Judgement

Overall, Donna is presented as a suffering citizen who is beginning to question the system. Perhaps the writer uses her to warn readers about societies that control people, and to show that hope and rebellion can survive.

Checklist: PEEL paragraphs, embedded quotations, methods named, one word zoomed in on, “because” to justify, a “However” paragraph, a final judgement, and the writer’s purpose.
